# Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved strong results in sequential decision-making problems, but its application to safety-critical domains remains difficult. In such domains, an agent must not only optimize long-term return, but also avoid actions that violate operational constraints during both training and deployment. This challenge is particularly evident in urban navigation, where an agent must reason about intersections, right-of-way, nearby traffic participants, and dynamic interactions while still progressing toward its destination. In this setting, pure task reward is not sufficient: a policy that reaches the goal quickly but behaves unsafely is unacceptable, while a policy that avoids failure by stopping indefinitely is equally undesirable.

These difficulties have motivated a broad line of work in safe reinforcement learning. One important direction is **shielding**, in which a safety mechanism constrains or modifies the actions proposed by a learned policy. Classical rejection-based shields can effectively prevent unsafe actions at execution time, but they introduce a mismatch between the policy used for acting and the policy used for learning. As a result, the learner may continue to update its parameters as if unsafe actions had been executed, even though the shield replaced or rejected them. This mismatch can reduce learning efficiency and weaken the policy’s ability to internalize safe behavior.

In this thesis, we study **Probabilistic Logic Shields (PLS)** and **Probabilistic Logic Policy Gradient (PLPG)** as a way to reduce this mismatch by integrating safety reasoning directly into the policy distribution. Instead of treating safety as a hard post-processing filter, a probabilistic shield assigns graded safety values to actions and reweights the policy accordingly. The resulting shielded policy is then used for both action selection and policy optimization. This formulation is attractive for two reasons. First, it provides a principled mechanism for balancing return and safety. Second, it enables safety specifications to be expressed in logic, which is especially useful in domains where constraints are relational, structured, and naturally symbolic.

We investigate this idea in the **LogiCity** simulator, a logic-driven urban navigation environment. LogiCity is particularly suitable for this study because the underlying task already contains the ingredients that motivate neurosymbolic safe RL: symbolic predicates, explicit rule-based safety conditions, and dynamic traffic interactions. Agents must reason about properties such as intersection occupancy, relative priority, collision proximity, and semantic traffic roles while following a route through the environment. This makes LogiCity a natural testbed for evaluating whether probabilistic logic shielding can improve urban safe path following beyond what is possible with a fully neural baseline.

However, transferring PLPG to LogiCity is not a trivial application exercise. The domain exposes a central tension that is easy to overlook in safe RL: **safety is not equivalent to useful behavior**. In our early experiments, highly protective shields could prevent failures while still producing poor task performance because the agent became overly conservative and timed out. This issue becomes even more pronounced under uncertain symbolic inputs, where a shield defined purely in terms of unsafe movement may collapse to repeated stopping. Consequently, the main question we address is not simply whether shielding reduces failures, but whether it improves **safe task completion**. In LogiCity, this must be evaluated primarily through **Trajectory Success Rate (TSR)** rather than safety metrics alone.

Against this background, we address the following two main research questions:

1. Does incorporating probabilistic logic shields improve **Trajectory Success Rate (TSR)** in LogiCity Safe Path Following?
2. Does incorporating probabilistic logic shields reduce the number of **training steps** required to reach strong performance?

To answer these questions, we compare unshielded PPO and PLPG-based agents in a structured set of experiments. Our study includes a main single-agent benchmark across task difficulties, an analysis of the safety-weight parameter, robustness experiments under noisy symbolic observations, a multi-agent shared-policy extension, ablations of shield design, and generalization experiments on larger maps and denser traffic scenarios. Together, these experiments allow us to evaluate not only whether PLPG works in LogiCity, but also under which assumptions, design choices, and operating conditions it remains effective.

We make three main contributions. First, we implement a PLPG-style probabilistic logic shield for Safe Path Following in LogiCity and evaluate it against a fully neural PPO baseline. Second, we show that shield design is a first-class methodological issue: a coarse shield that only distinguishes between stopping and moving can become over-conservative, while a reformulation based on **appropriate safe actions** better captures the need to balance safety and progress. Third, we extend the investigation beyond the idealized perfect-sensor setting by studying robustness to noisy symbolic inputs and by preparing a shared-policy multi-agent formulation in which several RL-controlled vehicles interact in the same logic-driven environment.

More broadly, we argue that the value of probabilistic logic shielding in urban navigation cannot be assessed by failure reduction alone. A shield is useful only if it helps an agent reach its destination safely and efficiently. For that reason, the contribution of this work is not merely the implementation of PLPG in a new simulator, but a structured empirical study of **when**, **why**, and **in what form** probabilistic logic shields support safe progress in a relational urban domain.
